# Professional Articulation and Executive Communication

**Course ID:** ART-101  
**Estimated effort:** 36–40 hours  
**Level:** Foundation to advanced professional practice  
**Audience:** Professionals, employees, graduates, corporate teams, technical specialists, managers, consultants and entrepreneurs  
**Assessment model:** Twelve modules, eight practical labs, recorded performance tasks, formative checks, capstone presentation and moderated Evidence of Capability Record

## Capability outcome

By the end of this course, you will be able to prepare and deliver clear, audience-centred professional communication in meetings, presentations, interviews, negotiations and executive briefings. You will demonstrate the capability through recorded practice, written artefacts, structured self-review, stakeholder feedback and a final portfolio.

## What competence looks like

A competent learner can:

- organise complex ideas into a clear message with a defined purpose;
- speak with suitable pace, projection, pronunciation, emphasis and pausing;
- adapt language and depth for executives, peers, customers and technical audiences;
- use evidence, examples and business stories without exaggeration;
- facilitate meetings and handle questions, objections and difficult conversations;
- deliver concise virtual and in-person presentations;
- give and receive professional feedback;
- produce an auditable portfolio showing planning, delivery, review and improvement.

## Before you begin

You need:

- a laptop or smartphone with a working microphone and camera;
- a modern browser that supports audio recording, or a separate recording application;
- a quiet practice area;
- a presentation tool such as Microsoft PowerPoint, Google Slides or equivalent;
- an approved storage location for recordings and assessment artefacts;
- one colleague, manager, mentor or peer who can provide structured feedback.

:::warning Privacy and organisational confidentiality
Never submit confidential customer information, personal data, contracts, credentials, financial records or proprietary material in course recordings. Use fictional or sanitised scenarios. Obtain consent before recording another person.
:::

## Course operating model

Each module follows a repeatable cycle:

1. **Understand** the communication principle.
2. **Plan** a message for a defined audience and outcome.
3. **Perform** the task in a realistic scenario.
4. **Review** the result using objective criteria.
5. **Improve** the next version.
6. **Prove** competence through evidence.

## Module 1 — Professional communication foundations

### 1.1 Communication is an outcome, not a transmission

Professional communication succeeds when the intended audience understands the message, knows what matters and can take the required next action. Sending more information does not automatically create clarity.

Use the **Purpose–Audience–Outcome–Evidence** model:

| Element | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Why am I communicating? | Secure approval for a pilot |
| Audience | What does this audience know and value? | Executive committee focused on risk and return |
| Outcome | What should happen next? | Approve a 30-day pilot and sponsor |
| Evidence | What supports the recommendation? | Cost, timeline, controls and expected benefit |

### 1.2 The communication loop

A complete loop contains:

- sender intent;
- encoded message;
- channel;
- audience interpretation;
- feedback;
- correction or confirmation.

Do not assume silence means agreement. Close the loop with a decision, summary, action owner or confirmation question.

### 1.3 Listening as a professional capability

Use active listening to identify facts, concerns, assumptions and emotions. Avoid preparing your response while the other person is still speaking.

A useful listening sequence is:

1. **Attend:** remove distractions and observe verbal and non-verbal signals.
2. **Clarify:** ask a neutral question.
3. **Paraphrase:** restate the key point in your own words.
4. **Validate:** acknowledge the legitimate concern or objective.
5. **Respond:** address the actual issue.
6. **Confirm:** agree on the next step.

## Practical Lab 1 — Baseline communication diagnostic

### Scenario

You are introducing yourself to a new cross-functional team. The audience includes managers, technical specialists and non-technical stakeholders.

### Tasks

1. Prepare a 90-second professional introduction.
2. Explain who you are, what you contribute, one relevant proof point and how colleagues should engage with you.
3. Record one take without stopping.
4. Create a transcript.
5. Review the recording for clarity, pace, filler words, posture, eye contact and strength of the closing statement.
6. Record a second version after making three deliberate improvements.

### Acceptance criteria

- the message has a clear beginning, middle and close;
- the role and contribution are understandable to a non-specialist;
- the second recording shows at least three documented improvements;
- no confidential or personal data appears in the submission;
- the learner records both self-assessment and external feedback.

## Module 2 — Voice, breath and delivery control

### 2.1 Breath supports the message

Shallow breathing can produce rushed speech, weak projection and tension. Before speaking:

- place both feet firmly;
- release shoulder tension;
- inhale low and quietly;
- begin speaking on a controlled exhale;
- pause at logical meaning boundaries rather than when breath runs out.

### 2.2 Pace

A useful professional pace is not one fixed number. Dense technical content generally needs a slower pace than a familiar story. Use pace to help the audience process meaning.

Review pace using three questions:

- Can the audience follow the sentence on first hearing?
- Are key terms given enough space?
- Does the delivery sound deliberate rather than memorised or rushed?

### 2.3 Projection and volume

Projection is supported resonance, not shouting. Adjust volume to room size, microphone placement and audience conditions. In virtual meetings, consistent microphone distance is more effective than speaking loudly.

### 2.4 Pause and emphasis

Pause before or after:

- the main recommendation;
- a numerical result;
- a contrast;
- a difficult question;
- the final call to action.

Emphasis should reveal meaning. Avoid stressing too many words in one sentence.

## Practical Lab 2 — Delivery control drill

Choose a 180–220 word business paragraph. Deliver it three times:

1. neutral pace;
2. deliberately slower with planned pauses;
3. conversational pace with emphasis on five key words.

Capture:

- duration of each version;
- words per minute;
- where pauses occurred;
- which version was easiest for a reviewer to understand;
- the final delivery standard you will use.

## Module 3 — Articulation, pronunciation and intelligibility

### 3.1 Articulation versus accent

Articulation concerns how clearly speech sounds are formed and connected. Accent is a legitimate pattern of pronunciation shaped by language, region and community. The goal of this course is **intelligibility**, not the removal of identity.

### 3.2 Common clarity barriers

- dropping final consonants;
- merging words when speaking quickly;
- reducing unfamiliar technical terms;
- inconsistent pronunciation of names and acronyms;
- insufficient vowel distinction;
- low volume at the end of sentences;
- speaking while looking down or turning away from the microphone.

### 3.3 Technical vocabulary preparation

Before a formal session:

1. identify names, acronyms and specialist terms;
2. verify pronunciation with an authoritative source or the person concerned;
3. write a phonetic cue that works for you;
4. practise the term in a full sentence;
5. decide whether the audience needs a plain-language definition.

### 3.4 Precision drills

Practise in short, controlled sets:

- final consonants: `risk`, `cost`, `scope`, `change`, `approved`;
- consonant clusters: `strengths`, `scripts`, `projects`, `controls`;
- number groups: dates, percentages, currency, telephone numbers;
- contrast pairs: `fifteen/fifty`, `can/can't`, `increase/decrease`;
- acronyms: say the full term once before using the abbreviation.

## Practical Lab 3 — Technical terminology briefing

Prepare a three-minute explanation of a technical concept relevant to your work. Include:

- five specialist terms;
- three acronyms defined on first use;
- one number or percentage;
- one plain-language analogy;
- a summary suitable for a non-technical decision-maker.

Record, transcribe and mark every place where a listener may mishear a term. Re-record until an independent reviewer can correctly identify all critical terms.

## Module 4 — Message structure and concise thinking

### 4.1 The headline-first principle

Busy stakeholders should not have to discover the point. Lead with the answer when the situation allows it.

A practical executive structure is:

1. **Headline:** the recommendation or status.
2. **Why it matters:** business impact, risk or opportunity.
3. **Evidence:** the minimum facts needed to support the point.
4. **Options:** realistic alternatives and trade-offs.
5. **Decision:** exact approval or action required.

### 4.2 The rule of three

Three well-organised points are usually easier to understand and remember than a long undifferentiated list. Group detail into three categories, phases, risks or decisions.

### 4.3 Signposting

Use verbal signposts:

- “There are three reasons.”
- “The immediate risk is…”
- “The recommendation is…”
- “The trade-off is…”
- “The decision required today is…”

### 4.4 Compression without distortion

Concise communication preserves meaning while removing repetition, unnecessary qualification and background that does not change the decision.

Do not compress away:

- material risk;
- uncertainty;
- assumptions;
- cost;
- ownership;
- dependencies;
- data limitations.

## Practical Lab 4 — Three-level message compression

Choose a real or fictional business proposal and create:

- a 15-second headline;
- a 60-second executive summary;
- a five-minute structured briefing.

All three versions must contain the same recommendation and must not contradict one another. Ask a reviewer to state the recommendation, top risk and requested decision after each version.

## Module 5 — Meetings and collaborative communication

### 5.1 Meeting purpose and role clarity

Every meeting should have a purpose, required participants, preparation material and an expected output. Common outputs include:

- decision;
- recommendation;
- issue resolution;
- status alignment;
- design review;
- action plan;
- learning or retrospective.

### 5.2 Opening a meeting

A disciplined opening includes:

- purpose;
- time available;
- agenda;
- decision rights;
- expected outcome;
- participation norms.

### 5.3 Contributing without dominating

Use concise interventions:

- add a fact;
- ask a clarifying question;
- identify a risk;
- connect two contributions;
- propose a decision;
- summarise agreement.

Avoid repeating a point already understood merely to gain visibility.

### 5.4 Closing the loop

End with:

- decisions made;
- unresolved items;
- action owner;
- due date;
- escalation route;
- next checkpoint.

## Practical Lab 5 — Facilitate a 15-minute decision meeting

Prepare and facilitate a simulated meeting with at least two participants or role-played stakeholders.

### Required artefacts

- one-page agenda;
- pre-read or issue statement;
- recording or observer notes;
- decision log;
- action register;
- self-review of facilitation quality.

### Acceptance criteria

- the purpose and decision are stated at the beginning;
- all participants have an opportunity to contribute;
- disagreement is handled without personal criticism;
- the meeting closes with decisions, owners and dates;
- the learner keeps the session within the agreed time.

## Module 6 — Executive presence and stakeholder confidence

### 6.1 Executive presence is observable behaviour

Executive presence is not a personality type. It is the combined effect of preparation, judgement, composure, clarity, credibility and follow-through.

Signals of professional confidence include:

- beginning without unnecessary apology;
- stating the recommendation clearly;
- acknowledging uncertainty without becoming evasive;
- remaining composed under challenge;
- separating facts from assumptions;
- avoiding exaggerated claims;
- committing only to deliverables you can control.

### 6.2 Non-verbal alignment

Your posture, facial expression, eye line and gestures should support the message. Avoid rigid performance. Aim for visible attention and controlled movement.

For virtual communication:

- place the camera near eye level;
- frame head and upper torso;
- use front lighting;
- minimise background distraction;
- look at the camera when delivering the main point;
- use the screen to read audience reactions without staring at your own image.

### 6.3 Credibility language

Prefer:

- “The current evidence indicates…”
- “The assumption we need to validate is…”
- “I do not have that figure with me; I will confirm it by 15:00.”
- “The decision has two material trade-offs.”

Avoid unsupported phrases such as “guaranteed”, “zero risk”, “everyone agrees” or “this is obviously the best option”.

## Practical Lab 6 — Executive briefing under challenge

Deliver a five-minute briefing recommending a change. Ask a reviewer to challenge you with at least five questions covering cost, risk, timeline, alternatives and failure conditions.

Your evidence must show:

- briefing plan;
- recording;
- question log;
- answers given;
- any answer that required follow-up;
- improved response after review.

## Module 7 — Business storytelling and persuasive evidence

### 7.1 Story is structure, not decoration

A business story helps the audience understand change, consequence and meaning. It should support the decision rather than replace evidence.

Use the **Context–Tension–Choice–Result–Lesson** structure:

- **Context:** what was happening;
- **Tension:** the problem, constraint or opportunity;
- **Choice:** what action was taken and why;
- **Result:** the measured or observed outcome;
- **Lesson:** what the audience should do now.

### 7.2 Ethical persuasion

Professional persuasion must not hide material facts or manipulate the audience. Distinguish:

- evidence from opinion;
- correlation from causation;
- forecast from actual result;
- representative case from exceptional anecdote.

### 7.3 Evidence hierarchy

Stronger communication usually combines:

1. verified data;
2. relevant example;
3. stakeholder observation;
4. clear limitation;
5. practical implication.

## Practical Lab 7 — Build a two-minute business story

Create a story about a project, customer, learning experience or operational improvement. Use fictionalised details where confidentiality applies.

The story must include:

- a clear tension;
- a decision or action;
- a credible result;
- one measurable proof point where available;
- a lesson tied to the audience's next action.

Record two versions: one for peers and one for executives. Explain how the emphasis changed.

## Module 8 — Presentation design and delivery

### 8.1 Start with the decision architecture

Before creating slides, write:

- audience;
- decision;
- core message;
- three supporting points;
- evidence required;
- likely objections;
- call to action.

### 8.2 Slide discipline

A slide should serve the audience, not function as the presenter’s script. Use:

- one clear point per slide;
- evidence labels and sources;
- readable contrast;
- limited text;
- diagrams for relationships;
- tables for precise comparison;
- charts for patterns and scale;
- speaker notes for detail not needed on screen.

### 8.3 Opening and closing

A strong opening establishes relevance and direction. A strong closing restates the decision and next step.

Avoid closing with “That is all” or an unstructured question mark. Use:

- recommendation;
- value or risk;
- decision required;
- next action and date.

### 8.4 Handling questions

Use the **Listen–Clarify–Answer–Confirm–Bridge** sequence:

1. listen fully;
2. clarify the question if needed;
3. answer directly;
4. confirm whether the concern was addressed;
5. bridge back to the main decision.

Do not invent an answer. Record and follow up on unknowns.

## Practical Lab 8 — Ten-minute professional presentation

Create and deliver a presentation with six to ten slides.

### Required content

- clear title and audience outcome;
- situation or opportunity;
- supporting evidence;
- options or trade-offs;
- recommendation;
- implementation or next step;
- closing decision request;
- question period.

### Quality gates

- slides meet accessibility and contrast requirements;
- spoken message is not a word-for-word reading of the slides;
- timing is between eight and twelve minutes;
- at least three questions are handled;
- sources and assumptions are disclosed;
- reviewer feedback is incorporated into a revised version.

## Module 9 — Difficult conversations, feedback and conflict

### 9.1 Separate observation from judgement

Use observable facts rather than labels.

Instead of: “You are unreliable.”  
Use: “The last three status updates arrived after the agreed deadline, which delayed the consolidated report.”

### 9.2 The SBI–Request model

- **Situation:** identify the specific context.
- **Behaviour:** describe the observable action.
- **Impact:** explain the consequence.
- **Request:** state the change or next action required.

### 9.3 De-escalation

When tension rises:

- lower pace and volume;
- acknowledge the concern without automatically agreeing with the conclusion;
- ask one question at a time;
- summarise common ground;
- separate immediate action from later investigation;
- propose a pause if productive dialogue is no longer possible.

### 9.4 Receiving feedback

Avoid defending every point immediately. Ask:

- “What did you observe?”
- “What impact did it have?”
- “What would a stronger version look like?”
- “Which change should I prioritise?”

## Module 10 — Interviews, career conversations and professional identity

### 10.1 Evidence-based answers

Use the **STAR–L** structure:

- **Situation:** relevant context;
- **Task:** responsibility or objective;
- **Action:** what you personally did;
- **Result:** measurable or observable outcome;
- **Learning:** what changed in your future practice.

### 10.2 The professional value proposition

A clear professional value proposition combines:

- capability;
- problem solved;
- audience served;
- proof;
- differentiator;
- next role or contribution sought.

### 10.3 Interview control

Prepare examples for:

- achievement;
- failure and learning;
- conflict;
- leadership;
- technical decision;
- ethical judgement;
- stakeholder management;
- prioritisation;
- change and uncertainty.

### 10.4 Asking strong questions

Ask questions that reveal priorities, operating context and success measures. Avoid questions easily answered by the public job description.

## Applied scenario — Recorded panel interview

Record responses to six questions. Include at least four STAR–L answers and one concise professional value proposition. Ask a reviewer to score relevance, evidence, ownership, clarity and concision.

## Module 11 — Cross-cultural, inclusive and accessible communication

### 11.1 Adapt without stereotyping

Culture can influence communication preferences, but individuals vary. Observe, ask and adapt rather than assuming.

Consider:

- direct versus indirect expression;
- turn-taking;
- formality;
- silence;
- hierarchy;
- time orientation;
- language proficiency;
- accessibility needs.

### 11.2 Plain language

Plain language supports experts and non-experts. It does not mean removing necessary technical precision.

Use:

- familiar words where they remain accurate;
- shorter sentences for complex decisions;
- defined acronyms;
- explicit ownership and dates;
- examples;
- summaries after dense sections.

### 11.3 Inclusive meeting practice

- distribute preparation material early;
- avoid unexplained idioms;
- use names correctly;
- allow processing time;
- provide captions or transcripts where practical;
- describe important visual information;
- make decisions visible in writing;
- invite contributions through more than one channel.

## Applied scenario — Global virtual briefing

Adapt a three-minute briefing for an international audience. Identify five changes you made to vocabulary, pace, examples, visuals, captions or interaction design.

## Module 12 — Professional communication system and continuous improvement

### 12.1 Build a repeatable system

Sustainable improvement requires a practice rhythm:

- one short recorded practice each week;
- one deliberate delivery focus at a time;
- one feedback conversation per month;
- quarterly portfolio review;
- pre-brief rehearsal for high-stakes sessions;
- post-session reflection on outcomes and audience response.

### 12.2 Personal communication scorecard

Track:

| Dimension | Evidence source | Example measure |
|---|---|---|
| Clarity | reviewer recall | audience states recommendation correctly |
| Concision | timing and transcript | message delivered within target |
| Delivery | recording review | controlled pace and pauses |
| Credibility | question handling | facts, assumptions and follow-ups separated |
| Engagement | audience response | questions and decisions captured |
| Improvement | version comparison | documented change between recordings |

### 12.3 Transfer to the workplace

For corporate learners, agree with a manager how competence will be applied. Suitable transfer tasks include:

- chairing a project meeting;
- presenting a status report;
- explaining a technical concept to a customer;
- delivering an onboarding session;
- conducting a stakeholder interview;
- presenting a business case;
- giving structured performance feedback.

## Capstone — Executive communication portfolio

### Scenario

You are proposing a practical organisational improvement to a mixed decision panel. The topic may concern customer service, process efficiency, training, security, technology adoption, employee experience or another approved professional area.

### Required deliverables

1. **Audience and stakeholder analysis** identifying knowledge, interests, influence, concerns and communication needs.
2. **One-page executive brief** with headline, evidence, options, recommendation, risks and decision required.
3. **Six-to-ten-slide presentation** designed for accessibility and decision support.
4. **Eight-to-twelve-minute recorded presentation**.
5. **Question-and-answer record** containing at least five challenging questions and responses.
6. **Two-minute business story** supporting the proposal.
7. **Meeting artefacts**: agenda, decision log and action register.
8. **Feedback evidence** from at least one reviewer.
9. **Revised presentation or re-recorded section** showing improvement.
10. **Reflective report** explaining strengths, limitations, ethical considerations and the next 90-day development plan.

### Assessment conditions

- the work must be the learner's own;
- sources, assistance and AI-supported drafting must be disclosed;
- recordings must not expose confidential or personal information;
- the recommendation must be supported by evidence and limitations;
- the final decision request must be explicit;
- the assessor may request a short verification interview.

### Assessment rubric

| Criterion | Weight | Competent evidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Message architecture and audience adaptation | 20% | Purpose, audience, outcome, evidence and decision are aligned |
| Articulation and delivery control | 15% | Speech is intelligible, paced, projected and deliberately emphasised |
| Executive presence and credibility | 15% | Composed, evidence-led communication; uncertainty handled transparently |
| Presentation and visual support | 15% | Accessible slides support rather than duplicate the spoken message |
| Question handling and interaction | 10% | Questions are heard, clarified, answered directly and followed up where needed |
| Business storytelling and persuasion | 10% | Story supports evidence ethically and leads to a relevant lesson |
| Meeting and written artefacts | 5% | Agenda, brief, decisions and actions are complete and usable |
| Evidence quality and improvement | 10% | Dated, sanitised artefacts show feedback, revision and capability growth |

### Competence decision

- **Competent:** overall score of 80% or higher, no critical privacy or integrity breach, and all mandatory evidence present.
- **Not yet competent:** missing mandatory evidence, unclear authorship, material confidentiality breach, or score below 80%.
- **Resubmission:** the assessor identifies specific gaps and the learner submits corrected evidence within the approved period.

- evidence of feedback and improvement;
- rubric calculation and competence decision;
- audit trail for resubmission or appeal.

## Corporate deployment guide

Organisations can use ART-101 for graduate onboarding, customer-facing teams, technical pre-sales, management development, trainer enablement and leadership programmes.

Recommended corporate implementation:

1. complete a baseline recording during onboarding;
2. assign a manager-approved workplace communication task;
3. provide monthly peer practice groups;
4. calibrate assessors against the rubric;
5. store evidence in a governed SharePoint, OneDrive or LMS location;
6. review transfer of learning after 30, 60 and 90 days;
7. issue the certificate only after moderation confirms competence.
